# Policy Limitations and Stress Test

The synthetic media policy developed for the University Communications Office establishes operational safeguards for responsible use, but no policy can eliminate every risk associated with synthetic representation. This section stress-tests the policy by identifying situations in which its rules may be misunderstood, bypassed, or become ineffective.

## 1. Ambiguous Boundaries and Human Judgment

Although the policy distinguishes between permitted, prohibited, and high-risk uses, some cases will not fit neatly into one category. Terms such as "materially altered," "sensitive subject matter," and "reasonable audience" require human judgment.

For example, an employee might create an AI-generated voice for a routine university announcement and reasonably consider it low risk. However, if the announcement concerns a controversial policy change or is delivered in a style associated with university leadership, audiences may interpret it as carrying greater institutional authority than the employee anticipated.

Two well-intentioned reviewers could therefore reach different conclusions about the same proposed use. The policy reduces this risk by requiring escalation when compliance is uncertain, but it cannot eliminate subjective judgment.


## 2. Consent Limitations

Documented consent is an important safeguard, but consent does not resolve every ethical concern.

A person may agree to synthetic use of their voice or likeness without fully anticipating how realistic the output will be, how audiences will react to it, or how easily the content may be copied and redistributed. The meaning of consent can also become unclear when content is reused later or placed in a different context.

For example, a faculty member might consent to a synthetic version of their voice for an instructional video but later object if an excerpt is reused in promotional material. The policy requires new permission for substantially different uses, but disagreements may still arise over what counts as a new use.

Consent therefore reduces risk but does not guarantee that every future consequence of synthetic representation was understood when permission was granted.


## 3. Disclosure and Context Loss

The policy requires clear disclosure, but disclosure can become separated from synthetic content after publication.

A university may correctly label an AI-generated audio clip on its website, but another user could download the file, remove the surrounding text, and repost a short excerpt on social media. An audience encountering the excerpt may then believe that the synthetic voice is an authentic recording.

This risk connects directly to my Task 6 experiment. My synthetic audio files were intentionally labeled as AI-generated, but that disclosure depended partly on how the artifacts were presented. If a file were renamed, clipped, or reposted without its original context, the audience would have fewer signals explaining its synthetic origin.

Embedding disclosure directly in media can reduce this problem, but no disclosure method can guarantee that the label will survive every transformation or redistribution.


## 4. Provenance and Detection Limitations

Provenance systems and AI-generated media detectors provide useful evidence, but neither should be treated as a complete solution.

In Task 6, I tested both of my synthetic audio artifacts using Hive Moderation's AI-generated audio detector. The detector identified the two artifacts as likely AI-generated with scores of approximately 99.3% and 99.2%. These results showed that detection can provide a useful signal, but my experiment involved only two artifacts and one detection system. It does not establish that the same detector would reliably identify every synthetic voice, tool, or future generation method.

Provenance mechanisms such as metadata and content credentials face a different limitation. They can document how content was created and modified, but this information may be lost through downloading, re-encoding, screen recording, editing, or reposting.

For these reasons, the policy treats detection and provenance as supporting safeguards rather than proof that content is authentic, truthful, or ethically acceptable.


## 5. Third-Party and Bad-Faith Misuse

The policy primarily governs people creating synthetic media on behalf of the University Communications Office. It cannot prevent an outside actor from creating unauthorized synthetic media about the university.

For example, someone outside the university could clone the voice of a university leader and create a false announcement. That person would not be following the university's consent, disclosure, provenance, or approval requirements.

A bad-faith actor could also take legitimate university synthetic media, remove its disclosure, edit the content, and redistribute it in a misleading form.

The university can respond through corrections, platform reporting, and incident-response procedures, but an internal policy cannot force outside actors to follow its rules. This is an important boundary of organizational governance.


## 6. Scale and Automation Risks

Synthetic-media systems can generate content much faster than humans can review it. This creates a tension between the efficiency that makes automation attractive and the oversight required by the policy.

For example, a system could generate thousands of personalized audio messages for prospective students. Even if the underlying template were approved, reviewing every generated variation could become impractical. Small factual, pronunciation, translation, or personalization errors could then be reproduced at large scale.

The policy requires additional review for large-scale automated distribution, but review itself may become a bottleneck. If production volume exceeds the organization's ability to conduct meaningful oversight, the safest response may be to reduce the scale of the project or refuse the synthetic approach.


## 7. Dependence on Good-Faith Compliance

Many safeguards in the policy depend on employees, contractors, and reviewers acting in good faith.

An employee could bypass the approved process by using a personal AI account, failing to disclose that synthetic media was used, or publishing content through an unofficial channel. A reviewer could also approve a project without examining the consent record or source information carefully.

Written rules cannot completely prevent deliberate circumvention or careless implementation. Training, access controls, clear responsibility, and periodic review would therefore be necessary if the policy were adopted in a real organization.

The policy creates expectations and accountability, but its effectiveness ultimately depends partly on whether the organization consistently enforces those expectations.


## 8. Residual Risk

Even when every requirement in the policy is followed, some risk remains.

A properly consented, accurately labeled, reviewed, and documented synthetic artifact may still be misunderstood by an audience. Disclosure may disappear during redistribution. A realistic synthetic voice may create an emotional impression that the written disclosure does not fully counteract. New generation techniques may also develop faster than detection, provenance, or organizational review practices.

The goal of the policy is therefore risk reduction rather than risk elimination. Compliance should not be interpreted as proof that a proposed use is harmless. When uncertainty or potential harm remains significant, the organization should retain the option not to use synthetic media at all.


## 9. Final Reflection

Stress-testing this policy changed how I think about synthetic-media governance. My Task 6 experiment showed how easily the same factual script could take on a different perceived personality and authority simply by changing the synthetic voice. It also showed that detection could identify my particular artifacts with high confidence, while still leaving unanswered questions about how reliable detection would be across other tools and future outputs.

The most important lesson from developing this policy is that no single safeguard solves the problem. Consent does not replace disclosure. Disclosure does not guarantee that context will survive. Provenance does not prove truthfulness. Detection does not establish intent. Human review can also fail, especially as production scales.

For that reason, responsible synthetic-media governance requires several safeguards working together and a willingness to refuse uses where the remaining uncertainty or potential for deception is too high. The policy provides a practical framework for making those decisions, but it cannot eliminate the need for human judgment.
